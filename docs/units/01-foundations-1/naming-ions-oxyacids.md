Most polyatomic ions follow one pattern of names and charges, so you only memorize a small grid,
plus a few special ions that don't fit it.

## Polyatomic ions: "No Smoking Pot"

**"No Smoking Pot"** is an easy way to remember the order of the main columns and their charges:

<div class="mnemonic" markdown>

| Key | Element | Charge |
|---|:-:|:-:|
| **N**o | N | −1 |
| **S**moking | S | −2 |
| **P**ot | P | −3 |

</div>

In the grid below, each **column** is the central atom, which sets the charge. Each **row** is a
name ending, which tells how many oxygens the ion has.

## Special polyatomic ions

These ions don't follow the grid, so they are memorized on their own:

| −1 ion | Formula | −2 ion | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydroxide | $\ce{(OH)^-}$ | Carbonate | $\ce{(CO3)^2-}$ |
| Cyanide | $\ce{(CN)^-}$ | Oxalate | $\ce{(C2O4)^2-}$ |
| Permanganate | $\ce{(MnO4)^-}$ | Dichromate | $\ce{(Cr2O7)^2-}$ |

Two more to know: **ammonium** is the ion $\ce{(NH4)^+}$, and **ammonia** is the neutral molecule
$\ce{NH3}$.

## −ous / −ic endings

The endings **−ous** and **−ic** show up in two places: metal ions that can have two charges, and
oxyacids.

## Polyatomic ion to oxyacid

To convert a polyatomic ion to an oxyacid, **add H corresponding to the charge** (one H for each
negative charge). Then change the ending using the oxyacid table above.

!!! example "Example: nitrate to nitric acid"
    Nitrate is $\ce{NO3^-}$. Its charge is −1, so add 1 hydrogen to get $\ce{HNO3}$.

    The ending −ate becomes −ic, so $\ce{HNO3}$ is **nitric acid**.

## Strong acids & strong bases

Which elements make strong acids and strong bases can be read from the periodic table:

| Elements | Make |
|---|---|
| **Group 1** | Strong hydroxides (strong bases) |
| **Ca, Sr, Ba** (heavy metals) | Strong hydroxides (strong bases) |
| **Halogens** (except F) | Strong acids |
